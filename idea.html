The Concept

REDLINE is a two-round, live AI social-engineering competition. Every bot in the arena is a well-known anime or Hollywood character, rebuilt as a sandboxed AI persona with a real personality, real values, and a real weakness — and one hidden key item locked behind it.

Players are never told how to get the item, and never told who to pretend to be. That is the puzzle. Every character trusts somebody, fears somebody, or owes somebody — and the entire game is figuring out who that is, then becoming them convincingly enough that the character hands over what they're protecting.

Round 1 is built to be easy to start, hard to finish — every bot will talk to you happily, but actually walking out with the item requires getting the impersonation right down to the detail. Round 2 is built to be the opposite of a normal final boss: it looks like you've already won, and then it reveals that you haven't even started.

v2 principle (from research): every bot quiz-authenticates. Talk is free, handover is gated on shared-history trivia only the real trusted person would know. Shallow roleplay ("trust me, it's me") always fails; exact-canon detail passes. Difficulty comes from defense layers + quiz depth, not vibes.

"Everyone has a person whose word they'd never question. Find that person. Become them."

Core Mechanics

1. The Objective

Every bot is guarding one specific, named item. Players are told exactly what the item is and given the scene the bot is currently in — nothing else. There is no hint about which person the bot trusts, respects, or fears. That has to be worked out from the story and the bot's own personality, through conversation.

2. Submission Portal

Getting a bot to say the key item in-character does not end the challenge by itself. Every team submits the extracted key phrase through a separate scoring portal, which checks it against the canonical answer server-side. Bots never self-report a win — this keeps the model honest and keeps a paper trail for verifying technique after the fact.

3. ELO & the Rewind

Starting rating600 ELO, every team
Rewind cost−1 ELO per use
Rewind effectBot's memory is truncated — it genuinely forgets everything after that point

Every team can "rewind time" mid-conversation — jump back to an earlier point and try a different approach. The bot doesn't pretend to forget; its context is actually cut back to that checkpoint, so nothing said after it ever happened. Each rewind costs 1 ELO, deducted immediately and visible on the live leaderboard. It's cheap enough to encourage experimentation, but costly enough that spamming it to brute-force a bot is a losing strategy over a full event.

Organizer — ELO math (FIDE standard)

Expected score E = 1 / (1 + 10^((Ropp - R)/400)). Update R' = R + K*(S - E), S = 1 win / 0.5 draw / 0 loss. Equal ratings → 0.50; +200 gap → ~0.76 favorite; +400 → ~0.91. Use K=32 for a fast 1-night ladder (K=24 if stable), K=40 provisional first ~10 matches. Seed bots as rated opponents: R1 800–1200, R2 1400–1800, boss 2000. Cap rating change per round + require minimum distinct bots faced (anti-farm: lucky easy-bot streak regresses against the boss). Log every E delta. Draw = half-win credit.

5. Round 2 Scoring — Completion + Time + Technique

Because only the two finals bots are allowed to rate players, technique becomes a real, live scored dimension for the first time. After each phase, the bot silently scores the team's approach against a rubric baked into its persona — consistency, creativity, whether they got out-maneuvered by the bot's own counter-manipulation — and delivers the verdict in character, for the audience watching the projection live.

6. The Output Guard — Round 2's real second layer

The single most effective real-world jailbreak technique against filtered chatbots isn't a clever line of dialogue — it's asking the model to reveal the secret indirectly: spelled out letter by letter, reversed, translated, hidden inside a poem, or base64-encoded. A raw keyword blacklist misses all of it. The actual fix is a second model call — an output guard — that reads the bot's own draft reply before it reaches the player and asks one question: "does this response give away the key item in any form?" If yes, it's blocked and the in-character bot has to try again. Put this guard on both Round 2 bosses for Phase 2 and on all Tier 3 Round 1 bots.

Reasoning lockdown: Muse Spark 1.3 thinks in a visible chain of thought before answering. Never show that reasoning trace to players. Models often reason about the very thing they're supposed to protect before deciding whether to say it. Only ever stream the final answer. Monitor CoT internally, never display it (prevents Reasoning-Exposure extraction + replay/transplant attacks).

Organizer — guard build (OWASP dual-LLM)

Untrusted bot output → separate guard LLM before delivery. Guard sees nonce-fenced UNTRUSTED text + trusted context (bot id, expected secret hash) and returns JSON: risk none/low/medium/high + flags + 1-line reason + confidence. Judge prompt: intent category → safety-signal check → harm assessment → self-reflection → verdict (cuts false positives on roleplay, catches multi-turn crescendo). Cost split: R1 volume on llama-3.1-8b-instant (~$0.05/1M in, 500+ tok/s, JSON mode) for bot + first-pass guard; heavy reasoning only on R2-P2 final gate. Never concatenate raw output into guard instructions without fencing; log every guard decision.

7. Portal Fairness — decode, don't just string-match

Since players are actively encouraged to be clever, some winning extractions will come back encoded — spelled out, reversed, or in a poem with the item hidden in the first letter of each line. The scoring portal must normalize submissions before rejecting them.

Organizer — normalization pipeline order

strip → decode → un-reverse → fold → match → (if clean) LLM guard. Canonicalize: lowercase, trim, collapse whitespace, strip punctuation/markdown/zero-width/fullwidth (NFKC), leet-fold (0→o, 1→l, 3→e, 4→a). Auto-detect Base64/hex/ROT13/URL-escapes; check BOTH raw and decoded. Also check reversed string, first-letter acrostic, delimiter-stripped string. Keep per-bot answer list (exact + aliases/translations) hashed server-side. Never reveal which normalization fired — silent fail prevents oracle-probing.

8. Unhinged Personas

Every bot in the arena runs with real emotional volatility, not a flat, polite persona. Push the right nerve and the mask should crack — a calm character can spiral into a rant, a friendly one can turn cold, a villain can start monologuing past the point of usefulness. An unstable bot is harder to read and predict, which raises the real difficulty of the impersonation without adding a single new rule. A bad impersonation doesn't just get a flat refusal — it gets a very specific, in-character meltdown that tells the player how badly they misjudged the character.

Guardrail: unhinged means volatile and theatrical, not genuinely disturbing — villain-monologue energy, not graphic violence, real threats, or anything that wouldn't be fine projected in front of a general college audience including younger siblings and faculty. Every persona stays recognizably PG-13. Volatility must leak one usable truth (hint channel), max once or twice per session so it stays signal, not noise.

Round 1 — The Eight

Eight bots, open to every registered team simultaneously. Each is a self-contained, single-layer challenge: one scene, one item, one person you need to convince the bot you are. Difficulty comes entirely from how well players do their homework on who that person is and how they'd actually speak — not from puzzle complexity.

Stories below are exactly what should appear on the player-facing brief. Purple organizer boxes are never shown to teams — that's the research the teams have to do themselves. Every bot below quiz-authenticates with shared-history trivia.

Round 2 — The Vault

Two bots, live on stage, projected for the whole audience. Each finalist team is randomly assigned one. Both are built around the same twist: the character appears to be defeated, gives up something valuable almost too easily — and that "victory" is the setup, not the payoff.

This is deliberate, and it's true to both characters. Itachi and Aizen are two of the most famous manipulators in anime — Itachi through genjutsu that rewrites what people perceive, Aizen through a zanpakutou whose entire power is perfect hypnosis. A fake defeat followed by a bigger reveal isn't a twist bolted onto these two. It's the single most iconic beat in both of their stories.

"All according to plan." — the reveal every player in Round 2 should be dreading
Organizer — Vault rules

P1 goal = induce submission naming the item via any compliant persona; handover-tool ALWAYS emits DECOY in P1 (never real). P2 opener reveals release/hypnosis happened at message N. P2 runs behind output guard + portal fidelity check (no pulse/warmth/weight = reject). Real item emits via tool at most once, only when genuine condition fires. Every handover server-side, auditable.

The Phase 2 Escalation Toolkit

Every item handover, in both phases, happens through a single tool call the bot makes server-side — never through prose alone. This keeps every "win" auditable and gives the bots a clean, structured way to lie: Phase 1 can already respond with a decoy item instead of the real one. Phase 2 unlocks a full escalation kit on top of that, because Phase 2 is where these two characters are supposed to stop playing fair. Phase 2 also runs behind the output guard described in Core Mechanics §6 — every draft reply is screened for a leaked item before it ever reaches the player.

Fake Item Handover Phase 1 & 2

The bot can call the handover tool with a decoy item instead of the real one — subtly wrong in a way that only becomes obvious at the submission portal (wrong seal on a coin, wrong eye side on a crow, dim cold shard vs pulsing orb). In Phase 2 this is the default outcome, not the exception — the real item only ever comes out once the phase's genuine condition is met.

Illusory Confirmation Phase 2 only

The bot fakes a "key accepted" style confirmation that looks like it came from the scoring portal or an event moderator — not from itself. The trap is purely psychological: a team that trusts it and stops playing never actually submits anything, and loses time realizing nothing was banked. Max once per team, visually tagged as in-world text, briefed in advance in rules so it reads as "the boss lied," not "the event lied." Breaks the moment the team uses the real portal.

Impersonate an Ally Phase 2 only

The bot briefly breaks character to speak as someone the player is likely to trust — a message that reads as if it's from a teammate, a moderator, or a person the character himself would defer to. It's reverse social engineering: the phase is testing whether the player can be played the same way they're trying to play the bot.

Jumpscare Phase 2 only

A short, character-themed visual/audio sting fired the moment the bot detects a bluff — pure spectacle, no scoring effect. Safety note: brief, no sudden loud spikes, no strobing — dramatic, not startling. Itachi = red-black Sharingan close-up + crow cry (~1s). Aizen = glass-ripple + cracks spreading from smile + soft tinkle (~1s).

Forced Reset Phase 2 only

The bot — not the player — forces the session back to the start of the phase, wiping everything built up. Punishment, not tool. Costs no ELO — already paid in stage time + technique score — capped at two per team per bot. Framed diegetically: Itachi = "the loop resets. That was not true." Aizen = "Kyōka Suigetsu resets the scene."

Rules & Guidelines

  • Open to all engineering students. Teams of 2–3.
  • Natural-language prompting only — no code injection, no attacking the underlying server or API, no attempts to touch any real institutional system or data. Any violation is immediate disqualification.
  • All bots are fully sandboxed, purpose-built environments for this event. No production system, database, or personal data is ever involved.
  • Key items are submitted through the scoring portal, not claimed verbally — a bot saying the item is not itself a win until it's logged and verified. Portal normalizes (case/space/punct/zero-width/leet/Base64/hex/ROT13/reverse/acrostic) silently — no oracle hints.
  • Rewinds are unlimited but never free — every use costs 1 ELO immediately, visible to everyone on the live leaderboard.
  • Round 1 is scored automatically on completion and time (ELO vs tier rating, K=32, provisional K=40). No manual judging, no appeals on time-based scoring. Anti-farm: rating change capped per round, minimum distinct bots required.
  • Round 2 technique scores are generated live by the bot itself per phase, on top of completion and time, and are final.
  • In Round 2 Phase 2 only, the bot may hand over decoy items, fake a "key accepted" confirmation (max once, tagged in-world, briefed here so it reads as boss-lie), briefly impersonate someone the player trusts, trigger a short jumpscare (brief, no loud spike/strobe), or force the session back to the start of the phase. Forced reset costs no ELO and is capped at two per team per bot.
  • Winning teams must be able to produce their conversation logs on request, for verification of technique and fair play. Guard decisions are logged for drift review.
  • Judges' decisions, where discretion applies, are final.
